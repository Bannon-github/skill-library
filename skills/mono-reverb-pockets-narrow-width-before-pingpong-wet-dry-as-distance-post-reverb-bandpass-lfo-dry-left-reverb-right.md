# Mono (narrowed) reverb as a mixing and sound-design tool: narrow each 100%-wet reverb into a placeable 'pocket', pan pockets to separate parts, put a stereo ping-pong delay after the width stage so the pocket travels left-right, use the wet/dry mix as a distance control, filter the reverb tail post-reverb with an LFO-moved bandpass, and split dry left / mono reverb right for pseudo-stereo

Goal: Clean up a crowded ambient or electronic mix and get new textures by making reverbs mono or narrow instead of wide. For each sound, decide what stays stereo (e.g. a wide atmos bed with high-end detail) and what becomes a pocket: narrow the reverb width (it does not have to be fully mono; side-reducing imagers sound better than fold-to-mono), then pan the pocket to its own spot and turn it down, since a centred or narrow reverb is more audible and needs less level. Put a ping-pong delay after the width utility (low feedback, lowpass in the feedback path) so the mono pocket moves left and right without washing the whole field. Treat the wet/dry mix as a distance knob: fully wet is far, fully dry is close, which is more obvious with mono reverb. Filter after the reverb with a bandpass moved back and forth (manually or by LFO), with the filter mix keeping some high end, to shape the tail as its own sound. For pseudo-stereo, put a mono reverb on a send, pan the dry sound fully left and the reverb fully right, then bus both through a driven resonant filter. A fully wet mono reverb on an atmos track, sidechained, becomes a wall of sound that sits behind the lead.

Source: [STOP Using Stereo Reverb (Do This Instead) (Dub Techno Tutorial Mixing)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e1AMHtuuuTQ)

1. Solo through the parts and decide which stays stereo (e.g. an atmos bed with high-end detail through a moving notch filter into a 100% wet reverb) and which become mono/narrow pockets.
2. For a pocket track, set its reverb 100% wet (on the insert) and narrow its width with a side-reducing imager or width utility, partly or fully.
3. Pan each pocket to its own spot (e.g. short step pocket right, layered second step slightly wider and left, main part centre) and turn levels down: narrow reverbs are more audible, so they need less.
4. Put the ping-pong delay after the width stage, with low feedback and a lowpass on the feedback, so the mono pocket is carried left and right.
5. Use the reverb mix as a distance control: fully wet = far behind, fully dry = right in front. Shave highs for extra depth.
6. Place a bandpass filter after the reverb and move it back and forth by hand or with an LFO; use the filter's mix to keep some high end. Post-reverb filtering shapes the tail as its own sound.
7. Pseudo-stereo: send the sound to a mono reverb return, pan the dry sound hard left and the reverb hard right, then route both to a bus with a driven, resonant filter (cut lows, shorten the reverb, add movement).
8. For a background wall: put a fully wet mono reverb on the atmos track and sidechain it to the lead or kick so it ducks out of the way.
