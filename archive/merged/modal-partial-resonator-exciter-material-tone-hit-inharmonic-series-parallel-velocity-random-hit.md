# Modal (partial-bank) physical-modelling voice: exciter into one or two resonators defined by a partial model, with material (decay vs frequency), tone (level tilt), hit (random partial emphasis), inharmonic stretch, parallel or series resonators, and velocity/random modulation for a playable mallet or string

Goal: Build a playable modal physical-modelling instrument from an exciter and a resonator made of tuned partials. Pick a partial model (string, beam, membrane, vibraphone, open tube or a manual partial list with 1 to 64 partials) and shape it with four relationships from the transcript: decay/release sets how long partials ring; material sets how fast high partials decay relative to low ones (muted vs glassy); tone sets partial level only (a static tilt, different from material); hit changes which partials are boosted at random, like plucking at a different position; and an inharmonic multiplier stretches or compresses partial spacing away from integer harmonics (metallic vs compressed). Choose the exciter (impulse, long exciter, noise): it mostly sets the attack, and a bass-heavy exciter mostly excites low partials. Run two resonators in parallel (blend) or in series (resonator 1 as an extended exciter, e.g. a short-decay drum head into a tube, with optional cross-feedback). Make it playable: velocity to level, material and exciter level; an envelope for a sharper attack over the ringing tail; subtle random on tone, stronger random on hit per note; key → layer gain to lift quiet high notes. More specific than our Karplus-Strong and comb-resonator skills: a modal partial bank with decay-vs-frequency and inharmonicity controls.

Source: [reFX 𝑹𝒊𝒑𝒑𝒍𝒆𝒓 Walkthrough - New Physical Modeling Synth!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pd9AFeTcl2k)

1. Mute the resonators and raise the exciter's direct output to audition exciters (impulse, long exciter, noise). Then turn the direct output back down and enable resonator 1.
2. Pick a partial model and partial count for resonator 1 (start with the string model). Try beam, membrane, vibraphone and open tube to hear how partial patterns change the instrument.
3. Shape the partials: decay/release for ring time; material down = high partials die faster (muted), up = the opposite; tone = cut highs or lows by level only; hit = random partial emphasis (pluck position); inharmonic multiplier up = stretched, metallic partials, down = compressed partials.
4. Optional manual mode: edit partial positions and amplitudes (e.g. 4 partials), make two snapshots and use a ratio control to morph between them.
5. Add resonator 2. Parallel: same exciter into both, set level and balance. Series: exciter → R1 → R2; use R1 with a very short decay (drum head) as an extended exciter for a tube in R2. Raise cross-feedback strength carefully.
6. Optionally layer the two basic oscillators and route chosen sources through two filters (series or parallel), e.g. filter only the oscillators. Use the second layer for a second, different sound.
7. Make it playable: velocity → resonator level, material (louder = more high partials) and exciter level; envelope 1 with a fast attack and low sustain/decay → level for a prominent attack over the tail; subtle random → tone; per-note random → hit; for a vibraphone, slow sine LFO → pitch at a very small depth.
8. Finish: low-cut a resonator if it gets muddy, then stereo widener, compressor, chorus and reverb. If high notes are quieter, map key tracking → layer gain.
